3. Detection

Drum Spike finds the hits for you. You start the analysis for the project: Hits > Analyze All, or per track with Hits > Analyze Selected Track. Adding tracks does not analyze on its own; the status line asks you to hit Analyze all. The detector listens for the role of the track only. A snare mic yields snare hits, not the hi-hat that leaks into it.

How it works

The detector looks for a sudden rise of energy in the bands of the drum itself. Gates then keep the real hits: the level, the shape of the hit against the loud hits of the track, and the attack in the time domain. Cross-mic rules decide the rest. A hit is loudest and earliest on its own mic, and the overhead mics deny hi-hat and cymbal bleed.

Steps

  1. Add tracks. Nothing analyzes yet: the status line ends with "hit Analyze all", and the empty track list says the same.
  2. Start the analysis: Hits > Analyze All, or Hits > Analyze Selected Track for the selected track.
  3. Only a part of a track: select a range in the wave lane and use Hits > Re-analyze Selection (Shift+R, also in the range context menu). The hits of the range are replaced, the hits outside keep their times, ids and velocities, and a new hit that would sit closer than the minimum gap to an outside hit is dropped. Edited hits in the range ask with the Keep dialog (it names the range); the run is one undo step and writes the backup of the previous events. The Inspector's Re-analyze button and Analyze Selected Track stay whole-track.
  4. The Sensitivity setting re-picks the hits from the kept candidate list. It does not run a new analysis, so it is fast and undoable. Analyze itself picks the hits at the track's stored sensitivity.
  5. Two detections closer together than the minimum gap merge into one, the earlier one wins. The gap is in milliseconds, 20 by default; the field is in the Inspector, section Detection.

The demo session holds 309 hits. That number counts the strokes of the demo kit; the hit count per track is higher where a stroke also reaches another microphone, because the same stroke is a hit on each mic that hears it. Chapter 4 shows where each one lands.
